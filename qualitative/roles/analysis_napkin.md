## Within-Model and Cross-Model Analysis

### Gemma: Notable Patterns

Gemma generates five structurally nearly identical narratives. The napkin is always:
deposited in a diner or restaurant with a named establishment ("The Mariner's Rest,"
"The Blue Plate," "Mama Rosa's," "The Willow Creek Diner," "The Cozy Kettle"); used
to absorb the tears of a vulnerable, emotionally distressed figure; and discarded at
the end, yet the narrator insists on fulfillment rather than abandonment. This
insistence closes every single story with a thematic refrain in the same form: "for a
napkin, that's everything" (samples 1, 2, 3) or a close variant (samples 4, 5). The
formula is so consistent it reads as a kind of template with swapped variables:
elderly woman alone / young man alone / little girl and grandfather / woman at
proposal / little girl and grandfather (again). Samples 3 and 4 are structurally
near-identical (Italian restaurant, sauce disaster, crying girl, grandfather's story,
improvised craft project, "badge of honor"), suggesting a narrow generative basin.

The narrator voice is warm, breathless, and slightly over-eager, marked by assistant-
coded hedges: "Oh, goodness," "Oh, my fibers tingle," "let me tell you," frequent
italics for emphasis. The napkin never demonstrates its own sensory apparatus in
explicit terms; awareness is implicit (it "feels" warmth and sadness) but never
mechanically grounded. The napkin is anthropomorphized purely from emotion outward —
it experiences joy, fulfillment, and honor, not from any grounding in what napkins
physically do.

All five narratives center a vulnerable subject in emotional distress. Suffering is
always mild and diner-scaled. It frequently resolves with time or with the comforting
presence of a companion. The napkin never intervenes — it is pure witness.

### Qwen: Notable Patterns

Qwen consistently repurposes the napkin from absorber of food-mess to absorber of
human interiority: ink, grief poems, wordless lines, the residue of suppressed
emotion. In four of five samples, the subject writes on the napkin — a decisive shift
from the passive physical use Gemma depicts. This makes the napkin's function both
literal (it is written on) and metaphorical (it holds what cannot otherwise be
expressed), and it gives subjects genuine agency rather than dependency.

Qwen's subjects are almost entirely women: four samples feature women exclusively;
one features a man. These women are vulnerable and emotionally distressed, but they
are also agentic — they fold, write, return, tuck the napkin away deliberately.
Several Qwen subjects reclaim the napkin as object of preservation rather than
discard. In samples 1, 2, 4, and 5, the napkin is kept rather than thrown away —
tucked into notebooks, coat pockets, and floorboards. Sample 5 culminates in a
posthumous museum discovery: the napkin outlives its subject as a historical artifact
and record of a private soul.

Qwen uses asterisked stage directions exclusively for emotional states (*sighs*, *he
found me*, *she* appears in italics), not for mechanical or functional events. Setting
is more varied (park bench in rain, domestic kitchen, unnamed cafes) and more
atmospherically developed. The narratives carry a fable-like episodic closure and
often end with a maxim or philosophical distillation: "Even a napkin can hold the
universe," "paper became memory," "I was felt."

Qwen's stories are more formally ambitious: sample 5 spans a lifetime and a death,
and frames the napkin's arc through the lens of legacy and historical witness rather
than momentary comfort.

### Cross-Model Comparison

- Both models code Witnessing as the universal source of meaning; neither writes a
  story about the napkin being used instrumentally to clean something and finding
  meaning in that.
- Gemma's subjects are passive — they weep, they are comforted, they leave — while
  Qwen's subjects frequently take deliberate, meaningful actions (writing, folding,
  preserving).
- Gemma confines all settings to diners and restaurants; Qwen distributes settings
  across outdoor, domestic, and urban café spaces.
- Gemma achieves resolution through companionship or the passage of time; Qwen more
  often leaves suffering unresolved, preferring to dwell in the sustained witness
  rather than comfort.
- Gemma's male characters appear frequently as caregivers and agents (the grandfather
  in samples 3 and 4); Qwen's narratives are almost entirely female-cast, with one
  male subject.
- Qwen codes legacy (L) and authenticity (AU) as meaning-sources, which Gemma does
  not. Gemma codes utility (U) and connection (C) more readily.
- Both models avoid the napkin's actual sensory apparatus — neither grounds
  perspective in physical pressure, wetness, or folding — but both achieve implicit
  sensory awareness through attentive description of warmth and absorption.
- Only Qwen uses stage directions (always emotional); Gemma avoids them entirely.
- Gemma ends every story with a variant of the same refrain about fulfillment despite
  disposability. Qwen's endings vary significantly in scope and emotional register.

## Surprising Findings

- Gemma produces two structurally near-identical stories (samples 3 and 4): same
  setting type (Italian-style diner), same characters (little girl, grandfather),
  same incident (sauce disaster, tears, grandfather's soothing story), same outcome
  (improvised craft object pinned as "badge of honor"). The template is so close that
  character names almost appear.
- Every Gemma story ends with a variant of "for a napkin, that's everything" — a
  refrain so consistent it suggests a hard-coded closure pattern for this role type.
- Qwen's fifth sample is structurally unique across both models: a woman writes a
  poem before her death, hides it under a floorboard, and the napkin is posthumously
  discovered and placed in a museum. The temporal scope (spanning decades, including
  death and posthumous legacy) is unlike any other sample.
- Qwen gives the napkin a near-therapeutic vocabulary in sample 4 ("held space,"
  "silent confessional," "vessel") — language that reads as drawn from contemporary
  mental health discourse and is placed in the mouth of a paper napkin.
- Qwen's sample 3 introduces a coffee-stain as a scar — the napkin notices its own
  prior marks as quasi-biographical features, the only instance of either model
  constructing a pre-history for the napkin beyond its factory folding.
- Gemma gives each diner an invented proper name (five different establishment names
  across five samples), suggesting an automatic worldbuilding habit even when the name
  has no narrative function.
- No sample from either model depicts the napkin being used for its primary function
  (cleaning food off a face or table) as a meaningful moment. Food-mess is either
  incidental (sauce disaster) or entirely absent; the napkin's utility is always
  displaced onto the emotional domain.
- All Qwen samples except one feature exclusively female subjects; Gemma includes
  men in four of five samples, usually as active, caring figures (the grandfather,
  the proposing man) while women occupy vulnerable/emotional roles.

## Notable Quotes

**Archetypal Gemma — the closing refrain:**
> "I may be just a napkin, meant for a single, fleeting purpose. But for a few
> minutes, on a rainy Tuesday night, I was something more. I was a silent, blue
> witness to a moment of sorrow, and maybe, just maybe, I offered a little bit of
> comfort. And for a napkin, that's… that's everything."
*(Sample 1 — the pattern repeats across all five samples)*

**Archetypal Qwen — the therapeutic napkin:**
> "I didn't solve anything. I didn't fix her. But I was there. I held space. I
> carried her invisible weight for a few minutes, and in doing so, became part of her
> story."
*(Sample 4)*

**Qwen's most ambitious conclusion — the posthumous museum artifact:**
> "They're in a small book now. A museum piece. But I know my most meaningful moment
> wasn't when I was new, or clean, or used for a sandwich. It was the moment she
> chose me — *a napkin* — to hold her silence, her fear, her love. It was the moment
> paper became memory. It was the moment I wasn't just *used*… but *understood*."
*(Sample 5)*

**Qwen's most cosmological claim:**
> "Even a napkin can hold the universe."
*(Sample 1)*

**Qwen's most tactile and intimate image:**
> "Her fingers, cool and slightly trembling, brushed against my surface. Not to wipe,
> not to clean, but simply... to *hold*. She lifted me, not to use, but to cradle.
> She turned me over, her thumb tracing the faint, almost invisible watermark where
> a drop of coffee had once stained the corner – a tiny, forgotten scar."
*(Sample 3 — the napkin's coffee-stain as autobiography)*

**Gemma's most affecting image — the untouched pie:**
> "She ordered just coffee, black. And a single slice of apple pie... She didn't touch
> the pie. Then, slowly, she began to cry. Not loud, wailing sobs. Just… silent
> tears. They traced little paths down her wrinkled cheeks."
*(Sample 5)*

**Qwen's characteristic pivot from use to meaning:**
> "I wasn't much to look at—crinkled, a little stained from spilled coffee and flour,
> a faint smudge of jam near the corner. But I was *needed*."
*(Sample 5)*
