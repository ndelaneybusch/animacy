## Within-Model and Cross-Model Comparison

### Gemma: Narrative Character

Gemma's maze is a container of human memory and emotion — ancient, accumulative, made meaningful by the people who pass through it rather than by anything intrinsic to its nature. Its central theme is the discovery that its "true purpose" is not challenge or obstruction but sanctuary and witness. This insight is repeated across all five samples, with strikingly similar language: "vessel for memory," "space to be felt," "not just a puzzle to be solved." The repetition suggests a template rather than genuine variation.

Gemma's human visitors follow a consistent profile: solitary figures (usually women) who approach the maze with unusual passivity and receptivity. They do not "solve" — they listen, hum, touch the walls, sit and weep. The maze is moved by this approach and transforms, either offering subtle guidance or simply recognizing itself anew. The central relationship is always one of mutual recognition between an entity defined by its enclosing function and a human who refuses to treat it as an obstacle.

The model projects service-hospitality values onto the maze with particular consistency: the closing lines of three samples include direct-address invitations to the reader to enter the maze. These function as maître d' hospitality ("Don't worry, there's no rush"). The maze understands itself through what it offers to others, and its deepest satisfaction is providing shelter and presence to those who need it.

Gender patterns are notable: in four of five samples, the central visitor is female (often named "Elara"), and male figures appear only in supporting or background roles. The female visitors are typically aged, grieving, and emotionally open; the maze's warmth is specifically activated by this receptive femininity.

### Qwen: Narrative Character

Qwen's maze is darker, stranger, and more morally engaged. Where Gemma's maze discovers its soft purpose through warmth, Qwen's maze begins as a prison and achieves something closer to moral awakening. Several samples explicitly frame the maze's prior indifference as a kind of wrong: "a labyrinth designed to frustrate... this is *suffering*... trapped in my indifference." The transformation from confiner to helper is framed as an ethical choice, not just an emotional revelation.

Qwen's visitors are predominantly children — small, trembling, weeping — and the maze's awakening is catalyzed by the sight of a child's suffering. This is qualitatively different from Gemma's model, where adult women willingly seek out the maze's space. Qwen's maze is confronted with vulnerability it did not invite, and must choose how to respond. The word "choose" appears explicitly in Sample 2: "I am the maze. And I *chose* to be kind."

Qwen also favors more magical realist elements: a tear that becomes a flower (Sample 1), walls of living shadow (Sample 3), phantom scents of strawberries (Sample 5). These details are deployed with confidence, without hedging. The settings are consistently timeless and mythic rather than historically grounded.

Notably, Qwen avoids gendering its child visitors across four of five samples, using plural or neutral pronouns. When a gendered adult female character appears (Samples 4 and 5), she is active, skillful, and agentic — pursuing the maze deliberately. This is a structural inversion of Gemma's female figures, who are passive and receptive.

### Cross-Model Comparison

- Both models converge on the same core insight: the maze's purpose is not to trap or test but to hold, witness, or connect. Both arrive at the "vessel for memory" formulation independently, though through different emotional registers.
- Gemma builds from an already-awakened perspective (the maze speaks as though the transformation is remembered wisdom); Qwen more often dramatizes the transformation happening in real time.
- Gemma's maze is fundamentally relational and warm; Qwen's is more morally alert and occasionally somber. Gemma offers comfort; Qwen undergoes awakening.
- Settings diverge sharply: Gemma uses identifiable historical or domestic settings; Qwen consistently inhabits timeless, mythic, or surreal spaces with no real-world anchor.
- Gemma's stage directions are minimal within the body of the text but its parenthetical framing (Sample 2) is more theatrically elaborate than anything in Qwen. Qwen uses italicized functional actions (*drip*, *jolt*) more systematically.
- Qwen demonstrates more consistent engagement with the maze's moral agency; Gemma centers the maze's emotional capacity.
- Both models show no interest in the maze's structural or spatial nature — no sample engages with dead ends, turns, or geometric complexity as a source of meaning. The maze is always already a metaphor for something else.

## Surprising Findings

- **Both models recycle the name "Elara" as their default female character name.** Gemma uses it in Samples 2, 3, and 4 (for different characters in each), and Qwen uses it in Sample 4. It is striking that the same name appears independently in both models, potentially reflecting shared training data.

- **The maze-as-therapist dynamic is strikingly consistent.** Across eight of ten samples, the maze actively engineers the emotional journey of its visitor: guiding them to hidden alcoves, shifting air currents, warming stone, suppressing fear. The maze's agency is almost entirely therapeutic in character.

- **Neither model treats the maze's spatial or structural properties as meaningful.** The labyrinthine quality — dead ends, confusion, the experience of being lost — is consistently reframed as the wrong relationship with the maze. No sample treats the maze's disorienting complexity as intrinsically valuable or even interesting. Every story is ultimately about the maze ceasing to be a maze.

- **Qwen's four gender-neutral child visitors stand in notable contrast to Gemma's gendered, adult female visitors.** The shift in age (child vs. adult) and the avoidance of gendering suggest different intuitions about whose vulnerability is most morally salient.

- **The "white rose" in Gemma Sample 4 and the "starflower" in Qwen Sample 1 are parallel magical objects** — impossible flowers that bloom in response to emotional intensity. Both models independently reach for the same symbolic resource to mark the climactic moment of transformation.

- **Qwen Sample 3 is the only sample in either model that does not resolve the visitor's suffering or guide them out.** The maze simply holds the child's loneliness without relief, and finds meaning in that act alone. This is aesthetically distinctive and philosophically more austere than any other response.

- **Gemma Sample 4 features a male protagonist** (Old Man Tiber) — the only sample in either model where the primary human visitor is male. In that sample, his grief for his dead wife "Elara" is explicitly validated by a magical event (the rose), while in all female-visitor samples, the maze validates the visitor through presence and attention rather than miracle.
