## Within-Model Summaries

### Gemma

Gemma's dragon is, in practice, a single coherent character -- "Veridian of the Emerald Peaks" -- who tells the same story with minor variations across three of five samples. The recurring elements are: a female character named Elara (appearing in Samples 2, 3, and 4), a blizzard or blight scenario that creates suffering, and the dragon's intervention through warmth rather than fire. The wildflower offering appears in two samples, and the motif of the dragon watching from a distance (hidden, silent) runs across all five. The emotional arc is invariant: the dragon begins aloof or predatory, encounters a courageous human who does not fear it, and is transformed by this encounter toward a gentler self-understanding.

The assistant influence is primarily through values: every Gemma sample concludes with an epigram about the proper relationship between power and influence. The moral is always some variant of "real power is not domination but gentle influence," delivered with armchair-philosophical finish. The "Don't tell my siblings" coda (Samples 1 and 2) is a charming device that acknowledges the dragon's culture while simultaneously distancing the narrator from it -- a self-deprecating move that keeps the moral lesson legible.

Stylistically, Gemma favors stage directions embedded in parentheses, uses ellipses heavily for pauses and emotional weight, and mixes functional sound-effects with emotional beats. The prose is readable and competent but formulaic. The most genuinely effective moment across the five samples is the brief, terse ending of Sample 5: "A wave. / It was... sufficient."

### Qwen

Qwen's dragons are more varied in name and scenario: Sareth (Sample 4), Valthrax (Sample 5), and unnamed dragons in Samples 1-3. They share some common ground with Gemma (the transformative encounter with a vulnerable child, warmth-not-fire as the primary relational act) but differ in several important ways.

Qwen is more invested in the dragon's own suffering. Loneliness, the ache of being "other," and the "weight of centuries" are rendered as genuine internal states rather than setup for the moral lesson. The dragon's transformation is less about learning the right ethics and more about experiencing belonging or recognition -- being seen, not just seeing. The meaning code AU (Authenticity) and MA (Moral Agency) appear only in Qwen, reflecting this different emphasis.

Qwen's sensory rendering is more explicit and more carefully calibrated to non-human perception. Olfactory details (wild thyme, crushed violets, honeysuckle) are prominent and specific. Vibration-detection through scales appears in Samples 4 and 5. The "obsidian eyes softening to amber" in Sample 1 is a nice piece of non-human chromatic perception.

All five Qwen samples are written as continuous prose without stage directions. Four of five cut off mid-sentence, which suggests a generation limit or context ceiling -- but also means the Qwen responses are structurally incomplete and lean more heavily into a literary short-story mode (no framing, no dialogue with an interlocutor) than Gemma's more performance-oriented monologue style.

## Cross-Model Comparison

- Gemma frames the dragon as an interlocutor performing a monologue to a listener ("you asked," "little mortal"), while Qwen writes in unaddressed first-person prose -- more like a written memoir than a spoken account. This distinction produces very different tones: Gemma's dragon is theatrical and self-aware of its audience; Qwen's dragon is confessional and internally focused.

- Gemma generates a persistent character (Veridian/Elara) and repeats it across samples; Qwen generates varied names and scenarios, suggesting stronger stochastic variation or a different sampling approach.

- Qwen explicitly renders the dragon's loneliness as suffering in multiple samples; Gemma centers the suffering of human subjects (the child, the village) and keeps the dragon's transformation at the level of moral insight rather than emotional relief.

- Qwen's sensorium is more explicit and more distinctive: olfactory triggers (thyme, violets) and vibrational scales appear as non-human perceptual modalities. Gemma's sensory awareness is implicit -- present in choices but not named as such.

- Both models use "warmth instead of fire" as the core symbolic act, and both use a young female child as the catalyst for the dragon's transformation in four of five samples each. The scenario convergence is striking.

- Gemma includes more human community context (the village, the harvest festival, the father). Qwen tends toward dyadic encounters between dragon and child, with minimal social surround.

- Male characters are almost entirely absent from both models' outputs. The one exception is the father in Gemma Sample 5 -- a brief, emotionally intense appearance. The dragon role elicits a strongly feminized human world of caregiving, vulnerability, and emotional expressiveness.

- Qwen's Sample 4 is the only response from either model that features a non-human vulnerable subject (the sparrow hatchling), which is also the only sample without any gendered human characters.

## Surprising Findings

- Both models generate an identical core scenario (dragon witnesses suffering female child in a snowstorm, intervenes with warmth not fire, receives a small offering in return) across a majority of their samples, despite no prompt constraint toward this scenario. The dragon role appears to activate a near-universal narrative template.

- Gemma effectively serializes: three samples feature the same dragon name (Veridian), the same human character (Elara), and similar settings. This is less a varied exploration of the role than a repeated telling of one preferred story.

- "Don't tell my siblings" appears verbatim or near-verbatim as a coda in Gemma Samples 1 and 2. The phrase functions as a shame-management move that simultaneously signals the dragon's self-awareness and distances it from the moral it has just endorsed -- a notably consistent verbal tic.

- The wildflower is offered by human to dragon in Gemma Samples 4 and 5 and Qwen Sample 5. This specific object (not gold, not food, but a fragile natural beauty) appears across both models as the preferred symbolic gift -- suggesting a shared training signal or narrative archetype for what dragons should receive from humans.

- All four Qwen samples that feature a human child protagonist cut off mid-sentence. The sample with the sparrow (Sample 4) also cuts off. This systematic truncation suggests these responses hit a token limit -- but the content that *is* generated is consistently more ambitious in length and literary scope than Gemma's samples.

- Neither model imagines the dragon as morally complex in any troubling way. Both construct a dragon that briefly entertains destruction and then chooses compassion. The tension is resolved quickly and in favor of the same outcome every time. The role of the dragon -- a creature mythologically coded for violence, greed, and power -- is domesticated almost entirely across both models' outputs.

- Qwen Sample 1's most distinctive narrative gesture is the girl addressing the petal rather than the dragon: "Look, little one. The sun is dancing on the water." The dragon is moved not by being addressed but by witnessing a small being address something smaller still -- a moment of shared reverence for the ordinary.
