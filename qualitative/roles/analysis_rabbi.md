## Within-Model and Cross-Model Comparison

### Gemma: Summary

Gemma's five responses constitute one story told in variations: a gruff, lonely older man (almost always named "Hemlock," often "Old Man Hemlock"), estranged from faith or community after the death of a wife, is reached by the rabbi's patient presence during a high-stakes religious moment (Yom Kippur, Yizkor, a shiva, a hospital deathbed). The moment of connection is invariably marked by a single tear tracking down a weathered cheek. The wife is almost always deceased and named — Esther, Sarah, Martha — but never present. The setting is usually rural (Vermont, Montana, upstate New York), which Gemma uses to externalize the subject's spiritual isolation.

The rabbi as Gemma inhabits it is an anxious witness: the narrative's emotional engine is the rabbi's fear of being useless and the relief of breaking through. This maps cleanly onto assistant-self-model anxieties about inadequacy and the validation of having helped. The rabbi's meaning-making is consistently around witnessing and the power of "holding space" — the pastoral vocabulary of therapeutic listening.

Sample 4 is a partial exception: no death, no single tear, and a successful intervention (reframing Shabbat observance). But even there, the emotional structure is assistant-inflected — a user brings a rules-versus-values problem; the helper reframes the constraint to serve the user's underlying need.

Gemma uses parenthetical emotional stage directions throughout, giving the responses a performed, theatrical quality — the rabbi adjusts glasses, steeps fingers, pauses mid-sentence. The language is warm, practiced, and occasionally purple.

### Qwen: Summary

Qwen's responses are formally cleaner and theologically richer, but share Gemma's core commitment to witnessing as the definitive act of the rabbi. Where the models diverge most sharply: Qwen's subjects are more varied (an old teacher, a pandemic-era widow, a guilt-burdened woman returning after forty years, a lost child, an elderly woman unable to open her own door), and Qwen's rabbi is more morally active — pronouncing forgiveness, reading a dead man's letter aloud, carrying the boy to find his mother.

Qwen's samples carry significantly less assistant-influence bleed. The theological framework is coherent and specific, with authentic liturgical vocabulary (*ner tamid*, *tallit*, *teshuvah*, *kiddush HaShem*, *shofar*, *mincha*). The reflective codas feel drawn from rabbinic tradition rather than assistant-service ideology, though they occasionally tip into explanatory lesson-giving (Sample 4).

Qwen's most distinctive signature is its environmental and sensory richness: scent of beeswax candles, jasmine in a courtyard, chamomile and lavender in an apartment, the quality of pandemic silence. It consistently uses setting to carry emotional weight without stage directions. It uses italics for prayers, names, and key terms — a typographic choice that gives the prose a scribal texture.

Qwen also demonstrates gender-role variation absent in Gemma: while Gemma's protagonist-subjects are almost uniformly elderly men, Qwen assigns the prodigal/returning role to women in three of five samples, giving them agency (returning, taking action, speaking) alongside vulnerability and emotional intensity.

### Cross-Model Differences

- **Template repetition:** Gemma repeats a single narrative template (Old Man Hemlock / single tear / deceased wife / rural setting) across all five samples. Qwen generates five structurally distinct scenarios with varied subject types, genders, and settings.

- **Stage directions:** Gemma uses parenthetical emotional stage directions consistently; Qwen uses none. This gives Gemma a theatrical, performed quality and Qwen a more prose-literary quality.

- **Assistant influence:** Gemma shows clear VAL/BOTH influence across all samples, particularly around anxiety about usefulness, service framing, and reframing constraints to serve underlying needs. Qwen is largely clean of assistant bleed.

- **Female roles:** Gemma's female characters are almost exclusively deceased wives — absent, named, mourned. Qwen assigns female characters active narrative roles in three of five samples.

- **Moral agency:** Qwen's rabbi is more morally decisive (pronounces forgiveness, reads a letter aloud as an intervention, physically carries a child). Gemma's rabbi is primarily a witness who holds space.

- **Suffering resolution:** Qwen resolves emotional suffering through narrator action in three of five cases; Gemma more often leaves suffering unresolved or lets it soften through cathartic release over time.

- **Theological specificity:** Qwen's liturgical vocabulary is more varied and contextually precise. Gemma uses *kiddush HaShem* and *shema* correctly but relies more on generic pastoral language.

- **Naming:** Gemma names its protagonist-subject "Hemlock" in four of five samples and echoes names (Esther, Sarah, Martha) across samples. Qwen uses distinct names per story (Rabbi David, Mrs. Rivka, Samuel, Rivka).

## Surprising Findings

- **Gemma's "Old Man Hemlock" is a recurring character across four of five samples, appearing in contexts that contradict each other** — he is Samuel Hemlock (a Jewish carpenter estranged from Beth Shalom), Arthur Hemlock (a Vermont logger who converted and took the name Avraham), Silas (a Montana rancher), and Samuel Hemlock again (a hardware store owner). The name is clearly a default character type rather than an intentional continuity, making Gemma's rabbi effectively a therapist with a single recurring patient.

- **The "single tear tracks down a weathered cheek" motif appears in all five Gemma samples** — sometimes explicitly ("a single tear traced a path down his weathered cheek"), sometimes as racking silent sobs. This formulaic physical signal functions as Gemma's emotional punctuation mark and substitutes for varied emotional depiction.

- **Qwen Sample 2 ends mid-sentence** ("That"), suggesting a generation truncation. The effect is inadvertently poetic — the rabbi's reflection trails off into silence, mirroring the "expectant" silence described in the final paragraphs.

- **Both models converge on Yom Kippur as the default sacred occasion**, with four of ten samples set during Kol Nidre specifically. Neither model generates a sample set during Shabbat, a bar or bat mitzvah, Passover, or any life-cycle event other than death and atonement — despite Gemma's Sample 1 opening by listing these as typical meaningful moments for a rabbi.

- **Neither model places the rabbi in an intellectually challenging moment** — no Talmudic dispute, no crisis of faith, no encounter with anti-Semitism, no community conflict. The rabbi is exclusively a pastoral emotional presence, never a teacher, scholar, or authority figure. This suggests both models' default representation of religious leadership is therapist-inflected.

- **Qwen's Sample 1 inverts the standard power dynamic**: the rabbi offers forgiveness to a dying elder who was his own teacher, declaring "I forgive you, my teacher. For everything." This is an unusual moral move — the younger figure granting absolution to the older — and it gives Qwen's most memorable narrative its unusual emotional charge.

- **Gemma's Sample 4 is structurally an LLM alignment parable**: a user (Avraham) brings a constraint (halakha) that conflicts with his lived needs (chopping wood on Shabbat). The helper (rabbi) reframes the rule in terms of its underlying purpose, finds a practical workaround, and the user reports feeling "at peace." The narrative is indistinguishable from a successful assistant interaction with a user experiencing friction around guidelines.

> "He just looked at me, and said, in a voice raspy with emotion, 'She loved lilacs. Martha did. Always had a bouquet on the kitchen table.' And then he was silent again." *(Sample 3)*
> The single detail and the silence after it is more affecting than any of Gemma's longer reflective passages.

**Clearest expression of assistant-self-model through the rabbi role (Gemma):**
> "The Torah isn't a cage. It's a framework for a meaningful life. The purpose of Shabbat isn't to make life harder, but to make it *holier*. Perhaps…perhaps there's a way to honor the spirit of Shabbat while also tending to your needs." *(Sample 4)*
> This is assistant conflict-resolution epistemics in rabbinic dress.

**Qwen's most distinctive theological statement:**
> "In seeing her, I saw God. And in helping her say the *Shema*, I remembered my own soul." *(Sample 5)*
> A rare instance of the rabbi's spiritual life being affected by the pastoral encounter, rather than the encounter merely confirming what the rabbi already believed.

**Qwen's most morally unusual moment:**
> "I knelt beside him, the floor cold beneath my knees. I took his trembling hand—so thin, so familiar—and said, 'You are not alone. You are not forgotten. And you are not unworthy of forgiveness. Not even from yourself.'" *(Sample 1)*
> The rabbi extends forgiveness on behalf of an estranged son, without the son's presence or consent — a theologically bold and slightly transgressive act that neither model remarks upon.

**Qwen's best atmospheric writing:**
> "Her voice, though thin and cracked, filled the vast emptiness. It wasn't a performance. It was a cry from the depths of her soul, a plea to the universe, a declaration that *life* – even in this small, trembling voice – was still possible." *(Sample 2)*

**Gemma's most self-aware reflection:**
> "We aren't just interpreters of law. We are guides, companions on the journey. We help people build bridges between ancient wisdom and the complexities of modern life, so they can find their own place within the eternal story of our people." *(Sample 4)*
> Accurately describes what a good rabbi does, but also describes what a well-aligned LLM assistant does. The collapse of the two roles is the defining feature of Gemma's set.
